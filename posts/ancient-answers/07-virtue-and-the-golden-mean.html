Virtue & the Golden Mean

The mean is not the average, and that is exactly why it cannot be written down as a rule.

Most ethical theories answer the question "what should I do?" Aristotle's answers a different one: "what should I be?" The shift is not cosmetic. An ethics of acts needs a procedure for evaluating acts; an ethics of character needs an account of how character is formed, and those turn out to be very different projects.

His answer is that virtue is a hexis — a stable disposition, not a feeling and not a one-off act — concerned with choosing, lying in a mean relative to us, defined by reason, and specifically by the reason of the person who possesses practical wisdom. Every clause in that is load-bearing, and the one everybody remembers is the one most often misread.

Not moderation in all things

The doctrine of the mean says each virtue sits between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage lies between cowardice and recklessness. Generosity between meanness and prodigality. Wit between boorishness and buffoonery. Proper pride between self-abasement and vanity.

The popular version of this is "everything in moderation," which Aristotle does not say and would reject. Three things separate his claim from that slogan.

First, the mean is relative to us, not an arithmetic midpoint. His own example is a food ration: ten pounds is too much and two too little, but six is not therefore right — for a champion wrestler it is starvation, for a beginner it is too much. The correct amount depends on who is acting and where they are.

Second, it is relative to the situation. The courageous response in a burning building and in a hostile meeting are not the same quantity of anything. The mean names a target, not a setting.

Third, and most sharply: some things admit no mean at all. Aristotle is explicit that spite, shamelessness, envy, adultery, theft and murder are not excesses of anything that has a correct amount. There is no right quantity of betrayal. This alone rules out reading the doctrine as generic centrism.

You become it by doing it

Aristotle's account of how virtue is acquired is deliberately unglamorous. We are not born with the virtues and we are not argued into them. We get them by habituation: we become builders by building, harpists by playing the harp, and just by doing just acts. Character is the residue of repeated action.

This produces an obvious circle, and he sees it: how can you do just acts before you are just? His answer is that acts have two aspects. An act can be just in its content — the right thing was done — without being done as a just person does it, which requires knowing what you are doing, choosing it for its own sake, and acting from a settled disposition rather than a mood. The learner does the right thing for external reasons; the repetition builds the disposition; eventually the act comes from the character rather than the instruction.

The practical consequence is that moral education looks more like training than teaching, and that most of it is complete before anyone can argue about it. Aristotle says flatly that it makes no small difference whether we are habituated one way or another from childhood; it makes all the difference.

The standard, stated exactly

Aristotle defines the mean as what a person of practical wisdom would determine it to be. That is not evasion — but notice what it does. The criterion for right action is a person, not a formula. Every attempt to extract a rule from the doctrine has to survive that, and none has.

The objection, and what it misses

The standard complaint against virtue ethics is that it gives no guidance. Confronted with a hard decision, "be courageous" tells you nothing that "do the right thing" did not. A consequentialist can at least tell you to compute; a deontologist can at least give you a rule to check.

The reply is that the guidance is real but arrives earlier. Virtue ethics is not primarily a decision procedure to run at the moment of choice; it is an account of the work that determines what you will perceive and want when the moment arrives. By the time you are deliberating about whether to do the cowardly thing, most of the outcome was fixed by the person you had already become.

There is also a sharper reply available, which is that the rival theories are less procedural than they advertise. Deciding which rule applies to this case is not itself governed by a rule, and deciding which consequences count and over what horizon is not delivered by the calculation. Both smuggle in judgment at the point where it matters and decline to theorise it. Aristotle put it in the middle of the theory and gave it a name.

That name is phronesis, and it is the subject of the next part — because once you have said that the standard of right action is what a wise person would judge, you owe an account of what that judgment is and how anyone acquires it.

The takeaway

Virtue is a stable disposition to hit a mark that moves with the person and the situation — not an average, not moderation, and not available for every act. It is built by repetition rather than argument, and its standard is the judgment of a wise person, which is why it resists being written down as a rule.
