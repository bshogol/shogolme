Stoicism vs Epicureanism

One goal, two opposite routes — and a school whose name now means the reverse of what it taught.

The two great schools of the Hellenistic world agreed on the question and disagreed on everything else. Both asked how a person can live untroubled in a world that does not care about them. Both answered with a complete system — physics, then ethics, then daily practice. And the systems are opposites at almost every point, which makes the pair the best available demonstration that "ancient wisdom" is not one thing.

Start with the word, because it has been inverted

"Epicurean" now means a person of refined and indulgent taste — the wine list, the tasting menu. The historical Epicurus lived on barley bread and water and wrote that a small pot of cheese was an occasion for a feast when he wanted to indulge. His school, the Garden, was famous for its plainness.

The inversion is not an accident of language drift; it was partly manufactured. Rival schools, and later Christian writers, found it useful to portray a philosophy of pleasure as a philosophy of debauchery, and the caricature outlived the texts. Almost nothing Epicurus wrote survives intact — three letters and some collections of sayings — so the caricature had a clear field for a long time.

Two routes to the same destination

Both schools take the goal to be a settled, untroubled life. The Stoics call the target apatheia, freedom from destructive passion; the Epicureans call it ataraxia, freedom from disturbance, along with aponia, freedom from bodily pain. Then they diverge completely.

Pleasure, correctly defined

The Epicurean claim that pleasure is the good survives only if you take his definition of pleasure, which is unusual and does most of the work.

Pleasure, for Epicurus, is fundamentally the absence of pain and disturbance — a state, not a stimulation. Once hunger is gone you are in the highest condition of bodily pleasure; adding delicacies varies the pleasure without increasing it. That single move converts hedonism into asceticism, because the cheapest reliable route to painlessness is to want little.

It comes with a classification of desires that is genuinely useful. Some are natural and necessary — food, shelter, safety, friendship; these should be satisfied, and they are easy to satisfy. Some are natural but unnecessary — fine food rather than food; these are acceptable if convenient and dangerous if depended on. Some are empty: wealth beyond need, fame, power. These have no natural limit, which is why they cannot be satisfied and reliably produce disturbance.

The Stoic reply is that this smuggles in a mistake. Making tranquility the aim makes your good depend on your circumstances remaining tolerable, and circumstances do not consult you. Their answer is to locate the good entirely in the quality of your own agency, which nothing external can reach — the position examined two parts ago.

The two fears

Both schools thought most human misery came from specific false beliefs, and they targeted different ones.

Epicurus went after the fear of the gods and the fear of death. His physics is entirely in service of this: atoms falling through void, combining and dissolving by natural process, with no divine management of human affairs. The gods exist but are perfectly tranquil and take no interest in us — a being that is troubled by our conduct would not be blessed. And death, he argues, is nothing to us: where death is, we are not, and where we are, death is not, so there is never a moment at which death is bad for anyone. The famous summary is the four-part cure: nothing to fear in god, nothing to feel in death, what is good is easy to get, what is terrible is easy to endure.

The Stoics targeted something broader: the judgment that anything outside your control is good or evil. Fix that and the fear of death goes with it, as one case among many, rather than needing its own argument.

The one place Epicurus made room for freedom

Strict atomism looked as deterministic as Stoic fate, which Epicurus regarded as worse than the old religion — he said it would be better to follow the myths about the gods than to be a slave to the destiny of the physicists, since myths at least leave hope of appeasement. His remedy was the clinamen, an uncaused slight swerve in the fall of atoms, which breaks the causal chain and leaves room for agency. It is the ancestor of every libertarian account of free will, and it is exactly the move the Stoics refused — a refusal that becomes the subject of part 16.

Withdraw or take the post

The practical difference is starkest here. Epicurus advised lathe biosas — live unnoticed. Public life brings competition, envy, obligation and exposure to the judgments of people who do not have your interests at heart. Friendship, which he rated the greatest means to a happy life, is to be found in a small community of chosen people rather than in a city's business.

The Stoics held that a rational being belongs to a rational whole and has roles inside it: citizen, official, parent, soldier. Withdrawing from them is a failure of nature, not a strategy for tranquility. Marcus Aurelius' notebook is the best evidence of what the position costs — a man repeatedly reminding himself, before dawn, that he is getting up to do the work of a human being.

Which is right depends on something neither school can settle for you: whether the world's disturbances are the price of participating in something worth participating in. But it is worth noticing that the choice is a real fork with real costs, and that "ancient wisdom" as usually retailed today quietly takes the Stoic side on engagement while taking the Epicurean side on where pleasure fits — a combination neither school would recognise.

The takeaway

Both schools wanted an untroubled life and disagreed about everything else: providence or atoms, virtue or pleasure, duty or withdrawal. Epicurus defined pleasure as the absence of pain, which turned hedonism into a discipline of wanting little — the opposite of what his name now means.
